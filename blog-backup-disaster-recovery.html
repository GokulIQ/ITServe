Business Continuity

A Practical Backup and Disaster Recovery Plan for Growing Teams

Sep 03, 2026ITServe Continuity Team5 min read

A reliable backup gives a business options. A disaster recovery plan tells everyone which option to use, who is responsible, and how quickly vital services must return. Growing teams need both before an outage puts customers and operations at risk.

Define what recovery means for your business

Start with two simple measures. Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is the amount of data you can afford to lose. Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is how long each system can be unavailable. A payroll platform and a marketing archive rarely need the same target.

Use more than one copy

The familiar 3-2-1 approach remains useful: keep three copies of important data, on two different media types, with one copy stored separately. Today, that separate copy should also be protected from accidental deletion and ransomware.

  • Classify systems by business impact before assigning backup schedules.
  • Use encrypted, monitored backups with protected retention.
  • Document application dependencies and restoration order.
  • Keep recovery contacts, credentials, and vendor details accessible offline.
A backup that has never been restored is only an assumption. Regular recovery tests are what make a continuity plan trustworthy.

Test the process, not just the files

Schedule test restores for individual files, key applications, and a representative system. Record how long each step takes and update the plan when people, software, or infrastructure changes. Testing also gives teams confidence when an actual incident occurs.

Keep communication clear during an outage

Decide in advance who declares an incident, who talks to staff and customers, and where status updates will be published. Clear communication protects trust while technical teams focus on recovery.
